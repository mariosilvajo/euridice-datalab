Every real dataset has holes. The dangerous mistake is treating them as a
nuisance to delete — because **the pattern of what's missing is itself
data**. Who refused to answer, and why, can matter more than the answers you
received.

You'll use **[missingno](https://github.com/ResidentMario/missingno)**, a
library built for one job: making missing data visible. Its matrix, heatmap
and dendrogram turn invisible gaps into obvious patterns — and in this
dataset they reveal a specific kind of person: the respondent who guards
their financial privacy and skips *both* money questions.

Then comes the part no real survey can do. Because our data is synthetic, we
can compare what the survey **reports** against what was **true** — and
measure exactly how badly the refusals distort the headline finding.

## Checkpoint

- What's the difference between **MCAR**, **MAR** and **MNAR** — and which
  one can't be fixed with statistics?
- The missingness heatmap showed two columns disappearing together at ~0.75.
  What kind of *person* does that pattern describe?
- The survey under-counted media distrust by roughly a third. Nothing was
  coded wrong — so what went wrong?
- `dropna()` deleted 32% of respondents. Who, specifically, did it delete?

## The takeaway

`dropna()` is an **ethical decision**, not housekeeping. The professional
habit is simple: look at missingness *before* you analyse, and always tell
your reader what was missing.
