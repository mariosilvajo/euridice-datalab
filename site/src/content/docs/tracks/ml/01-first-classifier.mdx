Machine learning sounds like magic until you've done it once. In the next
hour you will: frame a prediction problem, train a real model on 900
learners' data, test it on learners it has never seen — and then, in the
**ethics lab**, *measure* whether your model treats disadvantaged learners
fairly, and make the call professionals must always make: *should this
model be used?*

You will build the model the careful way — deliberately **withholding** the
sensitive attribute so it cannot discriminate. Then you will audit it and
discover that this standard precaution did not work, and why.

## Checkpoint

- Why must the test set stay unseen during training?
- Your accuracy was ~66% — what would a *useless* baseline score? (Always
  predicting "passed" scores about 61%, so how much is the model adding?)
- The model **never saw** `has_own_laptop` and still failed that group badly.
  Explain to a colleague why removing a sensitive column doesn't create
  fairness.
- It **passed** the four-fifths rule yet was wrong about nearly half the
  no-laptop learners. Why were both checks necessary?
- The four-fifths rule is a **US** standard and EU law sets no threshold at
  all. So who decides what counts as too unfair here — and on what basis?
- What did you write in your `reflect_submit` judgment — deploy, or not?

## Where this leads

The ethics lab is the DataLab's signature pattern: **build it → measure it
→ name it → judge it**. You didn't just hear that models can discriminate —
you measured *your own model* doing it, connected the finding to the EU AI
Act, and put your judgment in writing. That judgment is saved to your
DataLab **portfolio**, which the [capstone](/tracks/ml/03-capstone/) exports
as one file you can submit for a EURIDICE microcredential.

EURIDICE's dedicated *AI Ethics & Digital Humanism* course goes deeper on
all of it — bias, fairness metrics and their trade-offs, accountability,
and the EU AI Act. Technical skill and ethical judgment are one competence,
not two.
